Macbeth Key Quotes: 15 Quotes You Should Know for GCSE

Fifteen flexible, exam-ready quotations from Macbeth, with who says them, what they mean, and how to actually use them in an essay.

You do not need to memorise huge chunks of Macbeth to do well in your GCSE exam. Trying to learn entire speeches word-for-word usually wastes revision time and makes exam-day recall harder, not easier. What actually helps is a smaller collection of short, flexible quotations that can be adapted to several different characters, themes and possible exam questions.

This guide covers 15 quotations worth knowing well. For each one, you'll find who says it, when it happens, what it means, a key word or technique to zoom in on, a short analysis, the themes it could support, and a suggestion for how you might actually use it in an exam answer. Macbeth is in the public domain, so every quotation below has been checked carefully against the original text for accuracy.

The 15 Quotes

Quote 1 of 15

"Fair is foul, and foul is fair."

Who says it

The Three Witches

When it happens

Act 1, Scene 1 — the play's opening scene, before Macbeth has even appeared

What it means

Good and evil, or appearance and reality, are confused and reversed from the very start of the play — what looks fair (good) is actually foul (evil), and vice versa.

Key word / technique

Paradox and chiasmus (the mirrored, reversed sentence structure "fair...foul, foul...fair")

Analysis

The paradox unsettles the audience immediately, warning them that nothing in the play should be trusted at face value. The witches speak in riddles because deception and confusion are exactly what they represent.

How you could use it in an exam

A strong opening reference for any question on the supernatural or appearance vs reality, since it establishes the theme before the plot has even begun.

Quote 2 of 15

"I have no spur to prick the sides of my intent, but only vaulting ambition..."

Who says it

Macbeth

When it happens

Act 1, Scene 7 — alone, weighing up whether to murder Duncan

What it means

Macbeth admits he has no real justification for killing Duncan — no wrong done to him, no genuine grievance — only his own ambition driving him forward.

Key word / technique

Metaphor ("spur," "vaulting") — ambition as a rider urging on a horse, one that could easily overreach and fall

Analysis

The horse-riding metaphor is self-aware: Macbeth already senses that unchecked ambition can throw the rider rather than carry them forward. This moment of honest self-knowledge makes his choice to go ahead anyway feel more damning, not less.

How you could use it in an exam

Ideal for questions on ambition, since Macbeth directly names it as his only real motive, in his own words.

AmbitionFate vs free will
Quote 3 of 15

"When you durst do it, then you were a man."

Who says it

Lady Macbeth

When it happens

Act 1, Scene 7 — goading Macbeth after he says he will "proceed no further"

What it means

Lady Macbeth ties Macbeth's masculinity directly to his willingness to murder Duncan, implying that hesitation makes him less of a man.

Key word / technique

Conditional structure ("When...then") used almost as an emotional trap, plus the loaded word "man"

Analysis

Shakespeare uses Lady Macbeth to redefine "manliness" as ruthlessness rather than honour, and it works — Macbeth is more persuaded by this attack on his masculinity than by any political argument.

How you could use it in an exam

A key quotation for masculinity, and also useful for showing how Lady Macbeth manipulates Macbeth through language rather than force.

Quote 4 of 15

"Look like the innocent flower, but be the serpent under't."

Who says it

Lady Macbeth

When it happens

Act 1, Scene 5 — advising Macbeth on how to behave in front of Duncan

What it means

Macbeth should appear welcoming and harmless while secretly planning violence.

Key word / technique

Biblical/Edenic imagery — the serpent recalls the snake in the Garden of Eden, associating deception with original sin

Analysis

The contrast between "flower" and "serpent" captures the play's obsession with the gap between appearance and reality, and casts Lady Macbeth as the play's clearest architect of deliberate deception.

How you could use it in an exam

A go-to line for appearance vs reality, and for character questions on Lady Macbeth's calculating intelligence.

Appearance vs realityAmbition
Quote 5 of 15

"Come, you spirits that tend on mortal thoughts, unsex me here."

Who says it

Lady Macbeth

When it happens

Act 1, Scene 5 — immediately after reading Macbeth's letter about the witches' prophecy

What it means

Lady Macbeth asks supernatural spirits to strip away her femininity so she can act with the ruthlessness she associates with men.

Key word / technique

Imperative verb ("Come") and the invented word "unsex" — a command aimed directly at the supernatural

Analysis

Shakespeare presents femininity, in Lady Macbeth's own mind, as an obstacle to violence — a deeply troubling idea that reveals how completely she has internalised the play's link between masculinity and cruelty.

How you could use it in an exam

Strong for both masculinity and the supernatural, especially in questions asking how Shakespeare presents Lady Macbeth early in the play.

Quote 6 of 15

"Is this a dagger which I see before me, the handle toward my hand?"

Who says it

Macbeth

When it happens

Act 2, Scene 1 — alone, just before he goes to murder Duncan

What it means

Macbeth sees, or imagines he sees, a floating dagger pointing him toward Duncan's chamber.

Key word / technique

Rhetorical question, plus a hallucination used as a dramatic device

Analysis

Whether the dagger is real, supernatural, or a product of Macbeth's guilty mind is left deliberately ambiguous, which is exactly the point — the play never lets the audience be sure where fate ends and Macbeth's own psychology begins.

How you could use it in an exam

Excellent for fate vs free will, since it stages the exact moment before he commits to murder, and for the supernatural more broadly.

Quote 7 of 15

"Will all great Neptune's ocean wash this blood clean from my hand?"

Who says it

Macbeth

When it happens

Act 2, Scene 2 — immediately after murdering Duncan

What it means

Macbeth believes his guilt is so enormous that not even the entire ocean could wash the blood — and the crime — from his hands.

Key word / technique

Hyperbole — the exaggerated scale of "great Neptune's ocean" against a single stain of blood

Analysis

The imbalance between the vast ocean and the small physical stain shows that Macbeth's guilt is really moral and psychological, not something any amount of literal washing could ever fix.

How you could use it in an exam

One of the clearest single quotations for guilt, and a strong contrast to Lady Macbeth's response to the same moment.

GuiltViolence
Quote 8 of 15

"A little water clears us of this deed."

Who says it

Lady Macbeth

When it happens

Act 2, Scene 2 — the same scene as Quote 7, responding to Macbeth's guilt

What it means

Lady Macbeth dismisses guilt as something practical and easily removed, unlike Macbeth's overwhelming reaction moments earlier.

Key word / technique

Understatement ("a little water") — the deliberate opposite of Macbeth's hyperbole

Analysis

Placed right after Macbeth's ocean image, this line is one of the play's sharpest contrasts, and it's ironic: Lady Macbeth's guilt does not disappear — it resurfaces later in her sleepwalking, proving her underestimated it completely.

How you could use it in an exam

Pair this with Quote 7 to show how Shakespeare contrasts the two characters' relationship with guilt within a single scene.

Quote 9 of 15

"Thou hast it now: king, Cawdor, Glamis, all, as the weird women promised."

Who says it

Banquo

When it happens

Act 3, Scene 1 — alone, now suspicious of how Macbeth became king

What it means

Banquo privately acknowledges that the witches' prophecy about Macbeth has come entirely true, and strongly suspects Macbeth murdered his way to the throne.

Key word / technique

Listing ("king, Cawdor, Glamis, all") builds a sense of inevitability

Analysis

Banquo's suspicion, voiced privately rather than publicly, shows how fear and self-preservation stop even a fundamentally honest character from acting on what he knows — a quieter form of moral compromise than Macbeth's.

How you could use it in an exam

Useful for fate vs free will and for questions specifically about Banquo, since it captures his double role as both witness and near-victim.

Quote 10 of 15

"For Banquo's issue have I filed my mind."

Who says it

Macbeth

When it happens

Act 3, Scene 1 — after Banquo has left, planning his murder

What it means

Macbeth admits he has corrupted ("filed," meaning defiled) his own soul purely so that Banquo's descendants, not his own, might eventually be king.

Key word / technique

"Filed" — a pun/archaic term meaning defiled, polluted

Analysis

This is Macbeth at his most calculating: he now murders not out of desperation but to protect power he has already taken, showing how far ambition has moved from justification into pure self-interest.

How you could use it in an exam

Strong evidence for the corrupting effect of power once it is already held, not just the pursuit of it.

PowerAmbition
Quote 11 of 15

"Thou canst not say I did it: never shake thy gory locks at me."

Who says it

Macbeth (to Banquo's ghost)

When it happens

Act 3, Scene 4 — the banquet scene, in front of his guests

What it means

Macbeth, seeing Banquo's ghost at the feast, insists he cannot be blamed, even while visibly terrified.

Key word / technique

"Gory" — visceral, violent imagery made worse by the fact only Macbeth can see it

Analysis

Unlike Duncan's murder, which happened offstage, Banquo's ghost forces Macbeth's guilt into a public setting, and his loss of composure in front of his own court marks a turning point in how others begin to see him.

How you could use it in an exam

Ideal for guilt and the supernatural together, and for tracking Macbeth's psychological decline across the play.

GuiltSupernatural
Quote 12 of 15

"Out, damned spot! Out, I say!"

Who says it

Lady Macbeth

When it happens

Act 5, Scene 1 — the sleepwalking scene, near the end of the play

What it means

Lady Macbeth, sleepwalking, imagines she still has Duncan's blood on her hands and cannot remove it, no matter how hard she tries.

Key word / technique

Imperative commands and repetition ("Out...Out") showing desperation, not control

Analysis

This directly reverses Quote 8 — the guilt she once dismissed as trivial has returned overwhelming and uncontrollable, proving Macbeth's earlier hyperbole was closer to the truth than her own confidence.

How you could use it in an exam

One of the single best-known quotations for guilt, and essential for any question tracking Lady Macbeth's arc across the whole play.

Guilt
Quote 13 of 15

"He has no children."

Who says it

Macduff

When it happens

Act 4, Scene 3 — on learning Macbeth has had his wife and children murdered

What it means

Macduff's grief-stricken response implies Macbeth could never understand the scale of what he has taken, because he has no children of his own.

Key word / technique

Short, blunt declarative sentence — grief expressed through restraint rather than a long speech

Analysis

The brevity is deliberate: Shakespeare lets a single short line carry more emotional weight than a longer outburst would, and it sharpens Macduff's resolve into something more personal than political duty.

How you could use it in an exam

Useful for violence and its human cost, and for character questions on Macduff's motivation for revenge.

ViolencePower
Quote 14 of 15

"I have no words: my voice is in my sword."

Who says it

Macduff

When it happens

Act 5, Scene 8 — confronting Macbeth in the final battle

What it means

Macduff has moved past speech entirely — only violent action can now answer what Macbeth has done.

Key word / technique

Metaphor — the sword replacing language as a means of expression

Analysis

Macduff's decisive action restores order to Scotland, positioning him as the play's model of justified, controlled violence in direct contrast to Macbeth's murders.

How you could use it in an exam

Strong for kingship and violence, especially for questions on how order is restored at the end of the play.

ViolenceKingship
Quote 15 of 15

"Tomorrow, and tomorrow, and tomorrow, creeps in this petty pace from day to day..."

Who says it

Macbeth

When it happens

Act 5, Scene 5 — on hearing of Lady Macbeth's death, near the play's end

What it means

Macbeth reflects that life is repetitive, meaningless and pointless, later calling it "a tale told by an idiot, full of sound and fury, signifying nothing."

Key word / technique

Repetition ("tomorrow, and tomorrow, and tomorrow") mimicking the dull, unstoppable passage of time

Analysis

This is the furthest point of Macbeth's despair — ambition promised meaning and power, but has left him believing existence itself is empty, a bleak final judgement on everything he has done.

How you could use it in an exam

An excellent closing quotation for fate vs free will, ambition, or a whole-text question, since it reflects on the entire play from Macbeth's final perspective.

5 Quotes to Learn First

If you only have time to properly learn a handful of quotations, these five are the most flexible — each one can support more than one theme or character question.

"Fair is foul, and foul is fair."

Works for the supernatural, appearance vs reality, and as a strong opening reference for almost any whole-text question, since it sets up the play's central confusion before the plot even begins.

"I have no spur to prick the sides of my intent, but only vaulting ambition..."

Macbeth naming his own motive in his own words makes this the single most useful quotation for ambition, and it also opens up fate vs free will, since he chooses ambition with his eyes open.

"Look like the innocent flower, but be the serpent under't."

Covers appearance vs reality and Lady Macbeth's character in one line, and pairs neatly with almost any question about deception or manipulation.

"Will all great Neptune's ocean wash this blood clean from my hand?"

The clearest single quotation for guilt, useful on its own or contrasted with Lady Macbeth's "a little water clears us of this deed" for an even stronger answer.

"Out, damned spot! Out, I say!"

Instantly recognisable, works for guilt on its own, and is essential for any question that asks you to track a character's change across the whole play.

How to Analyse a Macbeth Quote

A reliable way to build a full paragraph from a single quotation is to work through six steps. Here's the method applied to "Out, damned spot! Out, I say!"

  1. Make a point. Shakespeare presents Lady Macbeth's guilt as something she cannot control, however much she once claimed she could.
  2. Introduce the evidence. In Act 5, Scene 1, sleepwalking and imagining blood on her hands, she cries, "Out, damned spot! Out, I say!"
  3. Zoom in on an important word. The repeated command "Out" shows her giving orders to guilt itself, as though it could simply be dismissed like a servant.
  4. Explain Shakespeare's method. The short, broken imperatives replace the fluent, confident language she used earlier in the play, showing that her composure has completely collapsed.
  5. Connect to a theme. This directly reverses her earlier claim that "a little water clears us of this deed," proving that guilt cannot be dismissed as easily as she once believed.
  6. Consider Shakespeare's purpose. Shakespeare may be suggesting that conscience cannot be permanently suppressed, however much a character tries to reason it away.
