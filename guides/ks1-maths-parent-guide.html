KS1 Maths: What Parents Need to Know

KS1 maths is really about building number sense — a genuine feel for how numbers work — rather than memorising calculations by rote.

It's tempting to think of maths progress as "knowing your number facts", but in Years 1 and 2 the bigger goal is understanding what numbers actually mean and how they relate to each other. A child who can instantly recall that 7 + 3 = 10 without understanding why is in a different, more fragile position than one who genuinely understands how the two numbers fit together — the second child can work out new facts they haven't memorised yet.

The Main KS1 Maths Areas

  • Number and place value — understanding what a two-digit number actually represents, in terms of tens and ones.
  • Addition and subtraction — using objects, number lines and mental strategies, moving gradually towards written methods.
  • Multiplication and division foundations — understanding equal groups and sharing, ahead of formal times tables in KS2.
  • Fractions — recognising simple fractions like halves and quarters of shapes and amounts.
  • Measurement — comparing and measuring length, mass and capacity using everyday units.
  • Money — recognising coins and notes, and working out simple totals.
  • Time — reading clocks and understanding units like hours, minutes and days.
  • Shape — recognising and describing 2D and 3D shapes and their properties.
  • Problem solving — applying number skills to short, real-life word problems.

Number Bonds Explained

A "number bond" is simply a pair of numbers that add together to make another number. The bonds to 10 are a good example:

7 + 3 = 10
6 + 4 = 10
5 + 5 = 10

Knowing these bonds fluently — without having to stop and work them out each time — frees up a child's attention for harder maths later on. If a child already knows 7 + 3 = 10 instantly, working out 27 + 3 or 70 + 30 becomes much faster, because the same underlying fact is being reused in a bigger calculation.

Parent tip

Bonds to 10 are usually the most useful place to start, since so much later maths quietly relies on them. Bonds to 20 tend to follow more easily once bonds to 10 feel secure.

5 Signs Your Child Is Building Good Number Sense

These are everyday signs to look out for, not a formal test:

  • They can work out a missing number bond, not just recall a memorised one ("what plus 4 makes 10?")
  • They notice when an answer looks obviously wrong, even before checking it properly
  • They can explain, in their own words, how they worked something out
  • They can solve the same problem more than one way, if asked
  • They apply number skills outside of maths lessons, like sharing out toys fairly

Missing one or two of these isn't a cause for concern — they tend to develop gradually and in a different order for each child.

10 Easy Ways to Use Maths at Home

  • Shopping — estimate the total before reaching the till, then check.
  • Cooking — measure ingredients together, or double a simple recipe.
  • Clocks — ask "what time will it be in half an hour?" during the day.
  • Counting — count anything and everything, from stairs to socks in the wash.
  • Sharing — split snacks or toys equally between family members.
  • Games — board games and card games are full of counting, comparing and simple addition.
  • Money — let your child handle real coins and work out simple totals.
  • Journeys — spot number plates, house numbers and road signs.
  • Building — construction toys are a natural way to explore shape and quantity.
  • Everyday questions — "how many more do we need?" or "which is bigger?" cost nothing and take seconds.

Common Questions

Should my child know their times tables in KS1?

Formal times tables recall is generally a KS2 focus. In KS1, the groundwork is understanding multiplication as equal groups and division as equal sharing, which times tables build on later.

My child can do maths on a worksheet but freezes with word problems — why?

This is very common. Reading a problem and working out which calculation it needs is a different skill from calculating itself. Talking through real-life examples out loud, before writing anything down, often helps.

How much home maths practice is actually useful?

Short and frequent tends to work best — a few minutes woven into daily life usually builds more genuine confidence than an occasional long session.
