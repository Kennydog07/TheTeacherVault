What Should My Child Know by the End of Year 2?

Year 2 builds noticeably on Year 1 rather than repeating it. Here's what tends to change, and how to help without turning home into school.

By Year 2, most children have moved from learning to read towards reading to learn — and a similar shift happens in maths, where number facts start being applied to real problems rather than practised in isolation. As with Year 1, this is a broad picture of typical progress, not a checklist your child needs to tick off by a certain date.

Year 2 English

  • Reading fluency — reading longer, less predictable texts with growing pace and expression, rather than word-by-word decoding.
  • Comprehension — answering questions about a text, including ones that need a little inference rather than a directly stated answer.
  • Spelling — a wider range of spelling patterns, plus a longer list of common exception words that don't follow regular rules.
  • Sentence writing — writing several connected sentences, often using joining words like "because" or "and then".
  • Punctuation — using capital letters, full stops, question marks and exclamation marks more consistently, with an introduction to commas in lists.
  • Vocabulary — using a wider, more varied range of words, and starting to notice word choices in books they read.

Year 2 Maths

  • Place value — understanding tens and ones within numbers up to 100, and beginning to work with slightly larger numbers.
  • Addition and subtraction — using mental strategies as well as written methods, including with two-digit numbers.
  • Multiplication and division — recalling the 2, 5 and 10 times tables, and understanding multiplication and division as repeated groups or equal sharing.
  • Fractions — recognising and finding simple fractions like a half or a quarter of a shape or amount.
  • Measurement — using standard units for length, mass, capacity and temperature.
  • Money — recognising coins and notes, and working out simple totals and change.
  • Time — telling the time to the nearest five minutes, and understanding units like hours and minutes.
  • Shape — describing the properties of common 2D and 3D shapes.
  • Simple statistics — reading and interpreting basic tally charts, pictograms and simple tables.
Parent tip

Year 2's common exception word list is longer than Year 1's, covering trickier spelling patterns like "because", "climb" and "improve". A handful practised regularly beats trying to learn the whole list at once.

From Year 1 to Year 2

The biggest shift is usually independence. Year 1 tends to involve a lot of adult support for reading, writing and calculating; Year 2 gradually expects children to apply the same skills with a little more confidence on their own — reading a slightly longer book without help, or working out a two-step maths problem rather than a single calculation. This doesn't happen overnight, and a wobble at the start of Year 2 while children adjust is completely normal.

How to Help Without Turning Home Into School

This is worth taking seriously, because Year 2 is exactly the point where "extra homework" can start to feel like a chore rather than genuine support. A few things that tend to work better:

Fold it into normal life: Reading a recipe together, working out change at the shop, or checking the time before leaving the house all count as genuine practice.
Follow their energy: Ten focused minutes when your child is willing beats thirty minutes of resistance.
Celebrate effort, not just correct answers: "You worked that out really carefully" builds more confidence than only praising right answers.
Leave room for downtime: Year 2 is still early primary school — plenty of unstructured play remains genuinely valuable.

Common Questions

Is Year 2 the year of SATs?

Some schools use informal Year 2 assessments as part of ongoing monitoring, alongside teacher assessment across the year. Your child's school can tell you exactly what, if anything, applies in their case.

My child was confident in Year 1 but seems to be struggling more in Year 2 — is that normal?

Yes, this is a common pattern, since Year 2 asks for more independence and slightly more complex thinking. A short conversation with the teacher can help clarify whether it's a normal adjustment or worth a closer look.

How many common exception words does my child need to know by the end of Year 2?

The Year 2 list is longer than the Year 1 list and includes trickier spelling patterns. Rather than memorising an exact count, it's more useful to focus on words your child is currently getting wrong, a few at a time.
