How to Help Your Child With KS2 Reading Comprehension

Practical, everyday ways to build reading comprehension at home — no worksheets required, just the right questions asked at the right moments.

Being able to read the words on a page and truly understanding what they mean are two different skills. A child can read every word in a paragraph accurately, out loud, and still struggle to say what actually happened, why a character did something, or what a tricky word meant in context. That second skill — comprehension — is what most reading questions at school actually test, and the good news is that it's a skill you can help build at home, in ordinary conversation, without needing to buy anything or set up a formal lesson.

What Is Reading Comprehension?

"Comprehension" covers several distinct skills, and it helps to know what they actually are, because each one can be practised slightly differently:

  • Retrieval — finding a fact that's stated directly in the text. Example: "What colour was the door?" — the answer is written down somewhere; the skill is finding it.
  • Inference — working out something the text doesn't say outright, using clues. Example: "How do you think the character felt?" — when the text never actually says "sad", but shows it through what the character does.
  • Vocabulary — working out what an unfamiliar word means from the words around it, rather than looking it up.
  • Prediction — using what's happened so far to sensibly guess what might happen next.
  • Summarising — retelling the main point of a passage in far fewer words than the original.
  • Sequencing — understanding the order events happened in, especially when a text jumps backwards and forwards in time.
  • Understanding the author's language — noticing why a writer chose a particular word or phrase, and what effect it creates.

5 Ways to Improve Reading Comprehension at Home

1. Ask questions while reading, not just afterwards. Pausing partway through a chapter to ask "What do you think is going on here?" keeps understanding active throughout, rather than testing it only once at the very end.
2. Ask "How do you know?" Whenever your child answers a question about a book, follow up with this. It's the single most useful question in this whole guide — more on why below.
3. Talk about unfamiliar words together. Instead of simply providing a definition, try: "What do you think that word means, based on what's happening in the story?" This builds the actual skill being tested, rather than just the answer to one question.
4. Predict what might happen next. Before turning the page, ask "What do you think will happen now?" There's no wrong answer here — the value is in the thinking, not in guessing correctly.
5. Ask for a short summary. "Tell me what happened in that chapter in two sentences" is a genuinely difficult skill — it forces a child to separate the important parts of a story from the interesting-but-minor details.

The Most Useful Question: "How Do You Know?"

If you take away only one thing from this guide, make it this question. Whenever your child gives an answer about a book — a fact, a feeling, a guess about what happens next — asking "How do you know?" pushes them to go back into the text and find the actual evidence for it, rather than just offering a general impression.

This matters because it's exactly what most reading comprehension questions are really asking for, even when they don't say so directly. A confident-sounding answer with no evidence behind it is a guess; the same answer backed up with "because it says here that..." is comprehension in action. Asking this one question, consistently, does more for reading skills over time than almost anything else on this page.

Retrieval vs Inference

These two skills are often confused, so here's a short original passage to show the difference clearly:

Tom stared at his muddy trainers by the back door and then at the clock on the wall. He grabbed a cloth from the kitchen drawer, wiped the floor quickly, and had just finished when he heard his mum's key turn in the front door.
Retrieval question: "What did Tom get from the kitchen drawer?" — the answer ("a cloth") is stated directly in the text. The skill is simply finding it.
Inference question: "Why did Tom clean the floor so quickly?" — the text never says he made a mess or that he didn't want his mum to see it, but the muddy trainers, the glance at the clock, and the rush to clean up before she arrived all suggest it. The answer has to be worked out from clues.

Notice that the inference question can't be answered by pointing to one single sentence — it needs your child to connect two or three details and draw a sensible conclusion from them.

Try This Tonight

A 5-minute activity for almost any book:
  1. Read one page or a short section together.
  2. Ask one retrieval question ("What did the character do first?").
  3. Ask one inference question ("Why do you think they did that?").
  4. Follow the inference answer with "How do you know?"
  5. Ask for a one-sentence summary of what you just read.

That's the whole activity — five short steps, no writing required, and it works with a picture book, a chapter book, a comic, or even a magazine article.

When Your Child Gets Stuck

It's tempting to simply give the answer when a child is struggling, but a few small prompts usually get them there themselves, which builds far more confidence:

  • Read the relevant sentence again, slowly. Sometimes the answer was missed simply because of pace, not understanding.
  • Look at the sentences just before and after. Answers to trickier questions are often nearby, even when they're not in the exact sentence being asked about.
  • Underline or point to any clues. Physically marking a detail (even just with a finger) helps separate the useful information from the rest of the page.
  • Explain any unfamiliar words first. A single unknown word can block an entire answer — check understanding of the vocabulary before assuming the comprehension itself is the problem.
  • Break the question into smaller parts. A question like "Why did the character change their mind, and how do you know?" is really two questions — answering them one at a time is often much easier.
