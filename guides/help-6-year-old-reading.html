How to Help a 6-Year-Old With Reading

Reading develops at very different rates from child to child. Here's what actually helps at home, and how to keep it enjoyable.

Six is an age where reading can look completely different from one child to the next — some are already reading short chapter books, others are still working steadily through phonics-based readers, and both are entirely normal. What helps most children, wherever they're starting from, is regular, low-pressure practice that stays enjoyable.

Reading Has Several Parts

It helps to know that "reading" isn't really one single skill — it's several skills working together:

  • Decoding — working out an unfamiliar word using phonics.
  • Fluency — reading smoothly, at a natural pace, without labouring over every word.
  • Vocabulary — knowing what the words actually mean.
  • Understanding and comprehension — following the story or information, and thinking about it.
  • Enjoyment — genuinely wanting to read, which keeps a child practising in the first place.

A child can be strong in some of these and still developing in others — a confident decoder isn't automatically a strong comprehender yet, and that's completely normal.

7 Ways to Help at Home

1. Read Together

Take turns reading pages or paragraphs, so your child gets a break from decoding every single word themselves.

2. Let Them Reread Familiar Books

Rereading a book they already know builds fluency and confidence — it isn't a step backwards, even if it feels "too easy".

3. Use Phonics When It's Useful

For an unfamiliar word, encourage sounding it out — but don't insist on it for every single word if your child already reads it on sight.

4. Talk About Pictures and Context

Pictures and the surrounding sentence can offer useful clues alongside phonics, especially for tricky or irregular words.

5. Ask Simple Comprehension Questions

Pause occasionally to ask what's happening, rather than saving every question until the very end of the book.

6. Discuss New Words

If a word comes up that your child doesn't know, a quick, simple explanation in the moment builds vocabulary naturally.

7. Keep Reading Enjoyable

Let your child choose books sometimes, even ones you'd consider "too easy" or a bit repetitive — motivation matters more than difficulty at this stage.

What Should I Do When They Get Stuck?

Getting stuck on a word is completely normal and doesn't need a single "correct" response every time. A useful sequence to try is:

  1. Give them a few seconds to have a go themselves.
  2. Offer a gentle prompt — "what sound does it start with?" or "look at the picture".
  3. If they're still stuck, tell them the word so the story can keep flowing, and come back to it another time if it keeps coming up.

Always demanding they "sound it out" can slow a story down until it stops being enjoyable; always simply telling them the word can remove a useful decoding opportunity. A mix of both, judged by how the moment feels, tends to work best.

Parent tip

If a book is causing frequent frustration, it's usually a sign to pick an easier one for a while, rather than pushing through. Confidence built on easier books transfers well to harder ones later.

Ask More Than "What Does This Say?"

Questions that go beyond simply decoding help build genuine comprehension. A few worth trying:

  • "What happened just then?"
  • "Why do you think that happened?"
  • "How is the character feeling right now?"
  • "What do you think might happen next?"
  • "Which part of the page tells you that?"

When Reading Becomes a Battle

If reading practice keeps ending in tears or arguments, it's worth stepping back rather than pushing through. A few calm, practical adjustments can help: shorter sessions, letting your child choose the book, reading side by side rather than testing them, or simply taking a short break from formal practice while keeping story time alive in other ways, like audiobooks or being read to. If you have ongoing concerns about your child's reading progress, your child's teacher is the best person to speak to, since they can compare it with what they see in class.

Common Questions

My 6-year-old reads well but doesn't seem to understand what they've read — is that normal?

Yes, this is a common pattern at this age. Decoding and comprehension are separate skills, and a strong decoder doesn't automatically have strong comprehension yet — it usually catches up with continued reading and discussion.

How often should we read together?

A little most days tends to work better than a long session once a week. Ten to fifteen minutes is plenty for most six-year-olds.

Should I worry if my child avoids reading altogether?

It's worth gently exploring why — a book that's too hard, or too babyish, are both common reasons. If avoidance continues despite trying easier or more appealing books, it's worth mentioning to your child's teacher.
