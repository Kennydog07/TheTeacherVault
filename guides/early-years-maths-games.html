10 Easy Maths Games for Early Years Children

Early maths doesn't need worksheets. These ten simple games use things you already have at home to build counting, number and shape skills through play.

Young children pick up an enormous amount of maths without ever sitting down to "do maths" — through counting stairs, sharing snacks, sorting toys and noticing which cup has more juice. These ten games take that idea and make it a little more deliberate, without needing any special equipment or a trip to the shops.

1. Count the Stairs

What you need: A staircase, or any set of steps.

How to play: Count each step out loud together as you go up or down, pointing to each one.

What it practises: Counting in order, and matching one number to one step (one-to-one correspondence).

2. Number Hunt

What you need: Nothing extra — just your surroundings.

How to play: Spot numbers on car number plates, house doors, clocks and food packaging. Say each one out loud together.

What it practises: Recognising written numerals in everyday life, not just on flashcards.

3. Snack-Time Counting

What you need: Any small snack — grapes, crackers, raisins.

How to play: Count out pieces onto a plate together before eating, touching each one as you count.

What it practises: Accurate counting of real objects, one at a time.

4. Which Has More?

What you need: Two small groups of anything — toys, buttons, pasta shapes.

How to play: Lay out two uneven groups and ask "which has more?" or "which has fewer?" before counting to check.

What it practises: Comparing quantities, not just counting them.

5. Make a Pattern

What you need: Objects in two or more colours or shapes — socks, blocks, cutlery.

How to play: Start a simple repeating pattern (red, blue, red, blue) and ask your child what comes next.

What it practises: Spotting and continuing patterns — an early building block for later maths.

6. Shape Hunt

What you need: Nothing extra.

How to play: Look around the house or on a walk for circles, squares, triangles and rectangles — clocks, windows, road signs.

What it practises: Recognising 2D shapes in the real world, not just in a picture book.

7. Put Numbers in Order

What you need: A few written numbers — on paper, magnets, or bath letters.

How to play: Muddle three or four numbers up and ask your child to put them back in the right order.

What it practises: Understanding that numbers have a fixed order, and recognising them by sight.

8. One More, One Less

What you need: A small group of objects.

How to play: Count out a group, then ask "what would one more look like?" or "what if we took one away?"

What it practises: An early sense of how numbers relate to each other, which supports addition and subtraction later.

9. Build a Tower and Count

What you need: Building blocks, cups, or stacking toys.

How to play: Build a tower together, counting each block as it's added, and predict how many it will take before it wobbles.

What it practises: Counting while doing, plus an early sense of estimating.

10. Shopkeeper Game

What you need: A few toys or household items, and pretend "money" (real coins, buttons or paper cut-outs).

How to play: Take turns being the shopkeeper and the customer, "buying" items for a small number of coins.

What it practises: Counting, exchanging quantities, and an early introduction to the idea of money.

How Long Should We Play?

There's no need to schedule "maths time" as a separate activity. Five to ten minutes, woven into things you're already doing — getting dressed, tidying up, having a snack — tends to work far better than a planned sit-down session, especially for younger children whose attention naturally comes and goes. If a game isn't landing on a particular day, it's absolutely fine to drop it and try again another time.

Common Questions

My child can count to 20 but gets muddled counting objects — is that normal?

Yes, very normal. Saying number names in order is a different skill from touching and counting real objects accurately, and the second one usually takes longer to become reliable.

Do these games work for children who don't enjoy sitting still?

These are designed to be played on the move — on the stairs, around the house, out on a walk — rather than at a table, which often suits more active children better.

Should I correct my child if they count wrong?

A gentle recount together ("let's check together") usually works better than a flat correction. Miscounting real objects is extremely common at this age and improves naturally with practice.
