What Should My Child Know by the End of Year 1?

A broad, practical picture of Year 1 learning — not a pass/fail checklist. Children reach these skills at genuinely different rates.

Year 1 covers a lot of ground, and it's easy to feel like you need an exact checklist to know how things are going. In reality, teachers assess progress across a whole year through everyday classroom work, not a single moment where a child is judged "ready" or "not ready". This guide gives you a general sense of what Year 1 typically covers, so you know roughly what to expect — not a test to measure your child against.

Year 1 English

English in Year 1 generally continues building on Early Years foundations, with a strong focus on phonics, alongside:

  • Phonics — building a wider range of letter-sound combinations, including common digraphs like sh, ch and ai.
  • Reading — reading simple, decodable books with growing fluency, and beginning to talk about what's happened in a story.
  • Writing — writing simple sentences, usually with support, using capital letters and full stops.
  • Spelling — spelling many words phonically, alongside learning a set of common "tricky" words that don't always follow regular patterns.
  • Speaking and listening — talking clearly about their ideas and listening to instructions and stories.

Year 1 Maths

Maths in Year 1 broadens number work considerably, along with an introduction to several new areas:

  • Number — counting, recognising and ordering numbers, generally within 100.
  • Addition and subtraction — using objects, pictures and simple written methods.
  • Early multiplication and division foundations — counting in twos, fives and tens, and sharing groups of objects equally.
  • Measurement — comparing length, weight, capacity and time using everyday language.
  • Shape — recognising and naming common 2D and 3D shapes.
Parent tip

This list is deliberately broad. Your child's teacher can always give you a clearer picture of exactly where your own child is within it — general guides like this one are best used as a rough map, not a scorecard.

10 Things You Might Notice Your Child Doing

Rather than formal assessment language, here are some everyday signs of Year 1 progress you might spot at home:

  • Sounding out unfamiliar words in a book, rather than guessing from the picture
  • Writing a short sentence about their day without much help
  • Counting objects around the house up to 20 or beyond
  • Working out simple sums using fingers, objects or a number line
  • Recognising and naming shapes in everyday objects
  • Retelling the main events of a story in their own words
  • Spelling common tricky words correctly, at least sometimes
  • Sharing objects into equal groups, like sharing sweets between friends
  • Using words like "longer", "heavier" or "earlier" to compare things
  • Asking questions about words, numbers or stories out of genuine curiosity

How to Help at Home

Short, regular moments beat long sessions almost every time at this age:

  • Read together most days, even briefly — enjoyment matters as much as decoding practice.
  • Use everyday maths — sharing snacks, counting stairs, checking the time.
  • Practise a small number of tricky spelling words at a time, rather than a long list at once.
  • Let your child explain their thinking out loud — it builds confidence and reveals what they understand.

Don't Worry About…

Handwriting that's still a little uneven — fine motor control keeps developing well beyond Year 1.
Occasionally reversing letters like "b" and "d" — extremely common at this age and usually resolves with time.
A child who reads fluently but struggles to answer questions about the story, or vice versa — these are related but separate skills that often develop at different speeds.
Comparing your child's pace to a sibling's or a friend's — every child's path through Year 1 looks a little different.

Common Questions

Is Year 1 the first year with formal assessment?

Year 1 includes the phonics screening check, usually taken in June, alongside ongoing teacher assessment across the year rather than a single formal exam.

My child seems behind in one area but ahead in another — is that normal?

Yes, this is very common. Children rarely progress at exactly the same pace across every subject and skill at once.

Should I be worried if my child isn't writing full sentences yet?

Writing tends to develop a little later than reading for many children, since it involves several skills at once — spelling, handwriting and forming ideas. If you have specific concerns, your child's teacher can give you the clearest picture.
