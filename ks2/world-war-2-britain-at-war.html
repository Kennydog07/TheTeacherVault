      Teacher information

      The key stage 2 history programme of study (National Curriculum in England) requires pupils to study an aspect or theme in British history that extends their chronological knowledge beyond 1066. The non-statutory guidance gives “a significant turning point in British history, for example, the Battle of Britain” as one example. This hub shows how the Second World War and the Battle of Britain can be taught within that requirement. Studying the whole of the Second World War is not compulsory, so please choose the sections that suit your planning. Designed mainly for Years 5 and 6, and accessible to confident Years 3 and 4 pupils. See the collapsible adult section near the end for curriculum links, sensitive-teaching guidance and the full answer key.

        Why did the war begin?

        Big question, careful answer. Historians talk about many causes. Here is a simple, honest outline for your age group.

        Germany and its leader

        In 1933 Adolf Hitler became the leader of Germany. His Nazi Party turned Germany into a dictatorship, which means ordinary people could not vote freely or disagree safely. The Nazi government wanted Germany to take over land belonging to other countries and treated Jewish people, and many other groups, cruelly and unfairly. Before the war, Germany took over Austria and parts of Czechoslovakia. Other countries hoped to avoid another terrible war.

        The invasion of Poland

        On 1 September 1939 German forces invaded Poland. Britain and France had promised to help Poland and asked Germany to withdraw. Germany did not, so on 3 September 1939 Britain and France declared war on Germany. Later that month Soviet forces also entered eastern Poland. This was the start of the war in Europe. The decisions to invade and begin the war were made by the Nazi government and its leaders.

        Axis or Allies?

        Axis and Allies were the two main sides. The Axis were led by Germany, Italy and Japan. The Allies began with Britain, France and Poland. Later the Soviet Union (from June 1941), the United States (from December 1941), China and many other countries joined them. The countries of the British Commonwealth and Empire, such as Canada, Australia, New Zealand, South Africa and India, also played huge parts. Choose a side for each country:

        Remembering the Holocaust

        During the war, the Nazi government and its collaborators carried out the Holocaust, the systematic persecution and murder of six million Jewish people and millions of other victims. This is a very sad and important part of history. We remember the victims with respect. If you have questions or feel upset, please talk to a teacher or trusted adult.

        Children’s experiences in Britain also included refugees: between late 1938 and September 1939, around 10,000 mostly Jewish children from Germany, Austria and Czechoslovakia travelled to safety in Britain on the Kindertransport.

        Life on the Home Front

        The Home Front means everyday life in Britain while the war was going on. Open each card, then try the question or activity inside.

        Different experiences: People did not all live through the war in the same way. It depended on where they lived (city, town or countryside), their background, race, nationality, disability, gender and personal circumstances. For example, women, children, refugees, people from the Commonwealth and people with disabilities could all have very different stories.

        The Battle of Britain

        A turning point in British history. In the summer and autumn of 1940 the fight was about who controlled the skies. You will become a radar plotter in a calm, safe operations room.

        Why did the skies matter?

        Germany had taken over much of western Europe. To invade Britain across the sea, it needed to control the air first, so that its ships and troops would not be stopped. If the RAF could keep the skies from being controlled by Germany, an invasion was too risky.

        RAF and Luftwaffe

        The RAF (Royal Air Force) is Britain’s air force. The Luftwaffe was the German air force. From July to October 1940 they fought over southern England and the English Channel. Ground crews, mechanics, plotters and many women in the Women’s Auxiliary Air Force (WAAF) kept the RAF working.

        Radar and the Observer Corps

        Radar uses radio waves to spot aircraft far away, before anyone could see or hear them. A chain of radar stations along the coast gave early warning. Radar was less good over land and at low heights, so the Observer Corps, thousands of volunteers on rooftops and in fields, watched, listened and reported by telephone.

        Many nations, one effort

        The air battle was not fought by Britain alone. Pilots and crews came from Britain, Poland, Czechoslovakia, Canada, New Zealand, Australia, South Africa, Belgium, France, Ireland, the United States and other countries. Pilots became known as “The Few”, a nickname from a speech by Winston Churchill in August 1940.

        Why was it significant?

        Germany did not win control of the skies, so its plan to invade Britain was put off. Britain could carry on in the war and, over time, work with allies. Historians call it an important turning point, though the war went on for almost five more years.
