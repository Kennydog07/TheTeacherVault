Themes

Key Macbeth Themes

Ambition

Macbeth's ambition is the engine of the whole play — first suggested by the Witches, then encouraged by Lady Macbeth, until it overrides his own doubts about murdering Duncan. Shakespeare shows ambition as dangerous not because wanting more is wrong in itself, but because Macbeth pursues it through violence rather than patience, and each murder he commits to protect his position creates new threats he then has to remove.

Guilt

Guilt affects Macbeth and Lady Macbeth very differently. Macbeth's guilt appears almost immediately — in hallucinations, disturbed sleep and paranoia — while Lady Macbeth insists guilt can be washed away, only for it to resurface far more powerfully in her sleepwalking scene. Shakespeare uses this contrast to suggest that conscience cannot be permanently suppressed, however confidently a character claims to control it.

Power

Power in Macbeth is shown as unstable once it's seized illegitimately. Macbeth becomes king through murder rather than succession, and spends the rest of the play trying to secure a position that never feels safe — ordering further killings, relying on fear rather than loyalty, and growing increasingly isolated from the nobles who once supported him.

The Supernatural

The Witches and their prophecies drive the plot from the opening scene, but Shakespeare leaves it genuinely ambiguous how much they control events versus simply revealing possibilities Macbeth was already capable of choosing. Macbeth's later hallucinations, including Banquo's ghost, blur the line further between supernatural influence and his own guilty mind.

Kingship

Shakespeare contrasts Duncan and Macbeth as two very different models of kingship. Duncan is trusting and generous but arguably too trusting to see the danger around him, while Macbeth rules through fear and violence. Malcolm's rightful restoration at the end of the play suggests Shakespeare's audience was meant to see legitimate, lawful kingship as something worth defending.

Violence

Violence escalates steadily across the play, from Macbeth's initial battlefield heroism through Duncan's murder to the killing of Banquo and Macduff's family. Shakespeare shows how one violent act creates the conditions that seem to justify the next, until violence becomes Macbeth's only remaining strategy for staying in power.

Masculinity

Lady Macbeth repeatedly links masculinity to ruthlessness, questioning Macbeth's manhood to push him toward murder, while Macbeth himself equates courage with violence throughout. Shakespeare complicates this idea through characters like Macduff, whose grief for his murdered family is presented as entirely compatible with — rather than opposed to — his masculinity and his resolve to act.

Appearance vs Reality

"Fair is foul, and foul is fair" announces this theme in the play's opening scene, and it runs throughout: Macbeth is told to "look like the innocent flower, but be the serpent under't," Duncan trusts a castle and a host who both intend to kill him, and the natural order itself seems to invert as the play's violence increases.

Characters

Key Characters

Macbeth

A brave, respected soldier whose ambition — once encouraged by the Witches' prophecy and Lady Macbeth — leads him to murder Duncan and seize the throne. He grows increasingly guilty, paranoid and violent as the play progresses, ordering further killings to protect a position he can never fully secure.

Lady Macbeth

Initially the more ruthless and decisive of the pair, driving Macbeth toward Duncan's murder and appearing to reject conventional femininity to do it. Her confidence collapses across the play, culminating in the guilt-ridden sleepwalking scene that reveals what she once claimed to have suppressed entirely.

Banquo

Macbeth's fellow general, who receives his own prophecy from the Witches — that his descendants, not Macbeth's, will be kings. Banquo is suspicious of how Macbeth gained the throne but, unlike Macbeth, resists acting on the Witches' words, making him a clear moral contrast before Macbeth has him murdered.

King Duncan

The rightful, well-regarded King of Scotland, presented as generous and trusting almost to a fault. His murder while a guest in Macbeth's own castle is treated by the play as a profound violation of both hospitality and the natural, God-given order of kingship.

Macduff

A Scottish nobleman who grows suspicious of Macbeth and eventually leads the rebellion against him. After Macbeth has his wife and children murdered, Macduff's grief becomes the personal motivation for the play's final confrontation, in which he kills Macbeth and restores rightful rule.

The Witches

Three supernatural figures whose ambiguous prophecies set the entire plot in motion. Shakespeare deliberately leaves it unclear how much they control Macbeth's fate versus simply revealing a path he was already capable of choosing for himself.
